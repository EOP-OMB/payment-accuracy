  Corrective_Actions_Proportion: OHS calibrated action intensity to the severity and
    volume of findings. Where unknown payments were driven largely by documentation
    gaps and data access barriers, OHS deployed enterprise‑wide training (covering
    >90% of participants) and a Data Validation Tool that materially reduced follow‑ups
    and accelerated completion, which was a proportional response targeting the largest
    error driver by volume. The FY2025 retest/final adjudication cycle concentrated
    effort on transactions with the highest risk and monetary exposure, producing
    an outsized impact by reducing the adjudicated rate from 10.29% to 0.84%. For
    recipients struggling with statutory compliance (technically improper payments
    and overpayments), OHS targeted high‑risk strata with intensive supports (e.g.,
    the Fiscal Integrity Forum), then scaled the model via the planned FAIR Forums
    and expanded 1:1 technical assistance, matching resource level to risk concentration.
    Process changes, such as lengthening the review window by 3–4 weeks and requiring
    use of the Data Validation Tool beginning January 2027, address systemic workflow
    delays and quality issues commensurate with their contribution to prior year error
    rates. OHS set a FY2026 reduction target of 9.87% and achieved a preliminary FY2026
    IP+UP rate of 2.52% ($309.83M), indicating that the intensity and scope of corrective
    actions are sufficient to drive statistically meaningful improvements. The planned
    FY2026 retest/final adjudication (October 2026) is expected to yield an additional
    ≥25% decrease, aligning effort with remaining residual risk.

  detail_IP_Causes: "The primary causes of improper payments in the Head Start program\
    \ stem from accounting‑related errors and documentation deficiencies in the financial\
    \ records of sampled recipients. In several cases, funds were charged to incorrect\
    \ accounts at the time of payment, and although recipients later corrected allocations,\
    \ the transactions were still deemed improper because corrections occurred outside\
    \ the tested period, making the original expenditure inconsistent with federal\
    \ requirements. These issues contributed to both overpayments and underpayments,\
    \ including cases where expenditures were reimbursed to the Head Start account\
    \ at a later date, creating technically improper payments. \n\nAnother major driver\
    \ of improper and especially unknown payments was the absence of required supporting\
    \ documentation. Even when reviewers could determine the likelihood that a payment\
    \ might be proper, the lack of complete documentation, such as proof of payment,\
    \ cost‑allocation details, or salary/fringe support, prevented OHS from making\
    \ a definitive determination. As a result, these transactions were classified\
    \ as unknown payments, consistent with federal guidance that requires full documentation\
    \ for validation. This pattern of documentation gaps aligns with the broader concern\
    \ that some recipients lacked financial management systems capable of producing\
    \ the required transaction‑level detail. \n\nA significant subset of unknown payments\
    \ arose from failure to access needed data, particularly cost‑allocation information.\
    \ In many instances, documentation did exist but could not be located or provided\
    \ during the review period, leading reviewers to classify these cases under “failure\
    \ to access data.” This inability to access critical source documentation prevented\
    \ verification of payment accuracy and directly contributed to the high proportion\
    \ of unknowns observed in earlier review cycles. \n\nFinally, some improper payments\
    \ were linked to statutory requirements not being met, resulting in technically\
    \ improper payments. These include payments made in the correct amount to the\
    \ correct vendor but processed in ways that did not fully comply with federal\
    \ requirements, such as missing approvals, incomplete certifications, or improper\
    \ timing of accounting entries. These types of errors reflect structural issues\
    \ in certain recipients’ fiscal processes, including inconsistent application\
    \ of federal standards, weak internal controls, or lack of automated safeguards\
    \ to prevent misclassification or delayed corrections."

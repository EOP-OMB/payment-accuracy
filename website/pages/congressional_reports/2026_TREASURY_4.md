    Answer: The Office of Capital Access’ (OCA) Emergency Rental Assistance (ERA)
      Program was determined to be susceptible to significant improper payments for
      the fiscal year 2023 Office of Management and Budget’s (OMB) Circular A-123,
      Appendix C assessment period.  However, in August 2022, Treasury determined
      that due to the short-term nature of the COVID-19 pandemic relief programs,
      quantifying the amount and rate of improper payments, assessing the root cause,
      and developing corrective action plans to reduce payment errors in the future
      would provide minimal value and be an ineffective use of resources. Therefore,
      for its COVID-19 pandemic relief programs Treasury decided it would not report
      an improper payment estimate in its Agency Financial Report or on paymentaccuracy.gov.
      These COVID-19 pandemic relief programs are on the three-year improper payment
      risk assessment rotation. In addition, because the ERA program has concluded
      all of its disbursements to primary recipients as of 9/30/2025, there is no
      basis for improper payment estimates going forward.  OCA is, however, conducting
      Single Audit reviews, compliance testing and data analysis of recipient submitted
      data, and desk reviews. The results from these activities are reported as part
      of Treasury’s consolidated overpayments outside of payment recapture audit amounts.

    Accountability: The IRS promotes accountability for reducing improper payments
      through regular oversight reviews, program monitoring, and collaboration across
      the bureau. Executive managers and program officials identify opportunities
      to improve payment accuracy and oversee corrective actions to strengthen internal
      controls and compliance. The IRS also works with external partners, including
      paid preparers and software developers, to improve filing accuracy and help
      prevent improper payments. Program results are regularly reviewed, and processes
      are updated to address emerging risks and improve payment integrity. However,
      achieving the statutory improper payment target will require significant changes
      to refundable tax credit programs, including legislative action.
    Description: 'The IRS implemented targeted corrective actions across its refundable
      tax credit programs to reduce improper and unknown payments. These efforts,
      completed or planned for completion by the fourth quarter of FY 2026, reflect
      a coordinated, data-driven approach to improving payment accuracy across the
      Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC), Additional Child Tax Credit (ACTC), American
      Opportunity Tax Credit (AOTC), and Net Premium Tax Credit (Net PTC) programs.


      To strengthen compliance, the IRS conducted its annual Dependent Database (DDb)
      meeting with stakeholders from Taxpayer Services, Small Business/Self-Employed,
      and Information Technology to review prior-year results and refine compliance
      filters used to select returns for examination. The IRS also leveraged cross-enterprise
      data sharing with federal partners, including the Office of Child Support Services
      (OCSS), the Social Security Administration (SSA), and the Centers for Medicare
      & Medicaid Services (CMS), to verify eligibility and identify discrepancies
      before refunds were issued.


      The IRS also continued providing annual due diligence training for tax professionals
      through the IRS Nationwide Tax Forums and filing season readiness webinars.
      These outreach efforts help improve understanding of refundable tax credit eligibility
      requirements, support accurate return preparation, and address improper payments
      caused by taxpayer or preparer error. Together, these actions demonstrate the
      IRS''s commitment to continuously improving payment integrity while minimizing
      burden on taxpayers.'
    Needs1: The IRS has established strong internal controls, data-driven compliance
      processes, and targeted outreach to reduce improper and unknown payments. However,
      statutory limitations, including limited math error authority and the lack of
      regulatory oversight of unenrolled paid return preparers, restrict the IRS's
      ability to prevent errors before refunds are issued. If provided with additional
      legislative authority, the IRS could more effectively leverage its existing
      systems, workforce, and compliance tools to improve payment accuracy. With those
      authorities in place, additional investments in compliance systems, staffing,
      and enforcement activities would likely yield benefits that outweigh their costs
      by preventing improper payments and reducing the need for post-refund recovery
      efforts.
    Needs2: 'The IRS has requested targeted investments to support payment integrity
      through enhancing compliance tools and technology modernization. The agency
      is prioritizing technology upgrades aimed at expanding automation, improving
      data integration, and enhancing system uniformity to better support audit selections
      and taxpayer data validation. These modernization efforts are designed to standardize
      data across the enterprise, strengthen analytics and reporting, and accelerate
      the delivery of digital services, which will ultimately improve the agency’s
      ability to prevent and detect improper and unknown payments.


      A key initiative is the continued expansion of the Enterprise Case Management
      (ECM) system. The IRS projected that by the end of calendar year 2025, approximately
      15,000 staff would be using ECM to reduce time and cost associated with critical
      compliance functions. Furthermore, during the 2025 filing season, the IRS used
      new identity theft filters that successfully identified and prevented issuance
      of approximately $1 billion in fraudulent refunds across more than 84,000 tax
      returns. These resource allocations reflect a strategic, data-driven approach
      to improving payment integrity while maximizing return on investment.'
    Sufficiency: 'Refundable tax credits (RTCs) present unique payment integrity challenges
      because they are administered through the tax system and do not operate like
      traditional payment programs with upfront eligibility determinations. As a result,
      the IRS must generally rely on taxpayer self-certification of eligibility at
      the time a return is filed, increasing the risk of improper and unknown payments.
      Contributing factors to improper payments of RTCs include the inability to verify
      certain eligibility requirements before refunds are issued, misreported income,
      and taxpayer misunderstanding of complex eligibility rules.


      To address these risks, the IRS has implemented a layered, data-driven approach
      that combines compliance, technology, and outreach. Annual Dependent Database
      (DDb) meetings help refine compliance filters used to better identify returns
      with a higher likelihood of errors before refunds are issued. The IRS also uses
      data-sharing agreements with federal partners, including the Office of Child
      Support Services, the Social Security Administration, and the Centers for Medicare
      & Medicaid Services, to verify key eligibility information and strengthen payment
      integrity. In addition, the IRS provides ongoing outreach and education to taxpayers
      and tax professionals to improve understanding of refundable tax credit requirements
      and promote accurate return preparation. Together, these efforts help address
      the root causes of improper payments while recognizing that achieving the statutory
      improper payment target would require significant changes to refundable tax
      credit programs, including legislative action.'

  - Agency: TREASURY
    Answer: The Child and Dependent Care Tax Credit (CDCTC) became refundable only
      for Tax Year (TY) 2021, under the American Rescue Plan Act of 2021 (ARPA), as
      part of theCOVID-19 pandemic assistance programs. The changes to the CDCTC were
      temporary, applying only to TY 2021. The short-term nature of this program limits
      the IRS’s ability to calculate improper payment estimates. To generate an improper
      payment estimate, the IRS needs to conduct comprehensive audits over a large
      sample of tax returns, requiring at least three years’ worth of data to ensure
      accuracy. However, because ARPA’s changes to the CDCTC were limited to TY 2021,
      there isn’t enough time or data to create an estimate before the refundable
      nature of this program ends. Accordingly, the credit has no basis for improper
      payment estimates. In August 2022 Treasury determined that due to the short-term
      nature of these programs, quantifying the amount and rate of improper payments,
      assessing the root cause, and developing corrective action plans to reduce payment
      errors in the future would provide minimal value and be an ineffective use of
      resources. Therefore, for its COVID-19 pandemic relief programs Treasury decided
      it would not report an improper payment estimate either in its Agency Financial
      Report or on paymentaccuracy.gov. However, these programs will remain on the
      three-year risk assessment rotation. CDCTC completed a risk assessment in FY
      26, after previously falling below the $10M risk assessment threshold in FY
      25, and was determined to still be susceptible to significant improper payments.

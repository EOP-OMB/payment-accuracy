SurveyData:
- Answer: Improper payments identified through recovery audits typically arise from
    insufficient or missing documentation to support claims, inaccurate billing, unsupported
    diagnosis codes submitted for risk-adjusted payment, incorrect eligibility determinations,
    and errors in coordinating benefits with other insurers. In FY 2026, Medicare
    Fee-for-Service recovery auditors identified overpayments primarily related to
    documentation deficiencies in hospital outpatient, skilled nursing facility, laboratory
    test, and hospice claims, and the Medicare Secondary Payer Recovery Auditor identified
    overpayments related to coordination of benefits errors. HHS resolves the conditions
    giving rise to these improper payments through targeted corrective actions, including
    enhanced provider education, prior authorization requirements for high-risk service
    types, expanded use of the Fraud Defense Operations Center to identify suspect
    billing patterns before and after payment, and Risk Adjustment Data Validation
    audits to address unsupported diagnoses submitted by Medicare Advantage Organizations.
    To recover overpayments, HHS and its contractors employ several methods, including
    issuing demand letters, offsetting future payments, requiring direct refunds from
    providers and plan sponsors, and using Treasury's debt collection and offset processes
    when debts remain unresolved. Medicaid, the Children's Health Insurance Program,
    and ACF programs recover disallowed costs through federal-state reconciliation
    processes, direct recoupments informed by Payment Error Rate Measurement reviews
    and program-specific eligibility audits, and the administrative disallowance process
    for grant-based programs, with HHS working closely with states and grantees to
    address the underlying conditions driving errors and prevent recurrence.

- Answer: Recovered amounts are generally returned to the program accounts or trust
    funds from which the improper payments originated, ensuring the funds continue
    to support their intended purposes in accordance with 31 U.S.C. § 3352(i)(2).
    In Medicare Fee-for-Service, a portion of recovered amounts is used to pay Recovery
    Audit Contractor contingency fees and cover program administration costs, with
    the remainder returned to the Medicare Trust Funds. For Medicaid and the Children's
    Health Insurance Program, recovery audits are operated by states, and the federal
    share of recovered amounts is returned to HHS through the standard federal-state
    reconciliation process and credited to the applicable federal accounts.

  Key: ara2_2

  Type: TEXT
- Answer: HHS determines that recovery audits are not cost-effective for certain programs
    due to low recoverable amounts, the presence of existing controls that already
    identify and mitigate improper payments, or the potential for adverse effects
    on vulnerable populations. For ACF-administered programs, including Head Start,
    Foster Care, and the Child Care and Development Fund, the administrative burden
    of conducting formal recovery audits would outweigh the limited recoverable funds;
    instead, HHS recovers identified overpayments through the disallowance process
    and state-level recovery activities, which are better suited to the grant-based
    structure of these programs. For the Advance Payments of the Premium Tax Credit
    program, established processes — including annual tax reconciliation between APTC
    payments and net Premium Tax Credit claims filed with the Internal Revenue Service,
    data matching with the Department of Homeland Security, and risk-based monitoring
    of agents and brokers — already recapture overpayments and reduce the need for
    additional audit activities; HHS will reassess cost-effectiveness once State-based
    Exchange measurement is incorporated and a full program baseline is established.
    In Medicaid and the Children's Health Insurance Program, states operate their
    own Recovery Audit Contractor programs and independently assess whether recovery
    audits are cost-effective based on program size, administrative capacity, and
    expected recoveries, with HHS reporting the federal share of state recovery audit
    results. Across these programs, HHS concludes that the cost and operational impact
    of formal recovery audits would exceed the benefits, making alternative recovery
    mechanisms a more efficient use of federal resources.
